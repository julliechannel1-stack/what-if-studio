In the first hour, nothing on the ground breaks, and that is the strangest part. Your kettle still boils, and your streetlights still buzz, and your dog still sniffs the gate, because the Moon never held your house together. It held the sea. Far away, on a dark beach, the water stops climbing the sand the way it always has, and the tide turns thin and tired, like a breath that cannot quite fill the lungs. Fishermen stare at harbour walls where the water sits lower than the marks their fathers painted, and crabs scuttle across mud that should still be underwater. You cannot see any of this from your backyard, and yet it is happening, all at once, on every coast on Earth. The ocean has lost its conductor, and the music is already going quiet, and the first people to notice are the ones who live by the water. The Moon never asked for thanks, and yet every harbour and every beach was running on its quiet pull, and now the bill for its absence is arriving in shallow water and stranded boats.
