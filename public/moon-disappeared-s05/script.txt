In the weeks that follow, the change creeps inland, into places you would never connect to the Moon. Salt marshes turn to cracked mud, and wading birds leave in restless flocks, and the fish that spawned on the highest spring tides find no flood to carry their eggs into the reeds. Farmers near the coast taste salt in wells that used to run fresh, because the sea no longer pushes and pulls in its old rhythm, and the groundwater shifts in ways nobody predicted. Your calendar still shows the month, but the month itself has lost its meaning, because the word month comes from the Moon, and the lunar cycle that once marked time for hunters and sailors and gardeners is gone. You flip the page, and the dates feel like labels on an empty jar, and you start to wonder what else the Moon was quietly doing while you never thought to thank it. The Moon was a clock, and a pump, and a calendar, and a gardener, all at once, and you are only now learning how many jobs it held,
